Find the Flipped Bits

Show the details
  • What you're paying for is what Shannon calls the equivocation: how unsure the receiver still is about what was sent, after seeing what arrived. At 1 flip in 100 it is −(0.99 log₂ 0.99 + 0.01 log₂ 0.01) ≈ 0.081 bits per bit — 8.1 bits per block of 100.
  • Shannon's §12 example: 1,000 bits per second with 1 error in 100 delivers 1,000 − 81 = 919 bits per second of information — not 990, because the receiver also has to learn where the errors are.
  • At 1 in 2 the arrivals tell you nothing: you pay about 100 questions for 100 bits. A coin flipped at the receiver would do as well.
  • Your questions play the part of Shannon's “correction channel” (§12, Fig. 8). He proved it needs at least the equivocation — no question-asking scheme can do better on average.
How do I play?
  • Drag across some bits (or tap one) to pick a group, then ask whether any of them flipped. With nothing picked, you ask about all the bits you haven't cleared yet.
  • A no clears the whole group. A yes marks it orange: a flip is in there somewhere — split it and ask again.
  • When a single bit must be the flip, it flips back on its own. A block is done when every bit is cleared or fixed.
